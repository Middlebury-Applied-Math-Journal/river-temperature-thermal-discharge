# Bailey Parent 

## What are we optimizing for? 

That question follows me everywhere. 

From my studies in math, I am no stranger to optimizing. I have become fluent in the language of objective functions, inputs, and constraints. Even my decision to study math came from a kind of optimization: if I could choose one major, which would leave the most doors open afterward? Math seemed to maximize opportunity. But my earliest optimization problems were not in math. As an Eagle Scout, I learned you don’t only optimize a pack to make it light for you; you optimize it so the group can ultimately finish the trek. I learned that the best solution is not always the one that benefits a singular person, rather it is the one that makes the system more survivable for everyone in it. That lesson shaped the way I entered math, even though it was not an obvious path for me at first. I came to college intending to be a Classics major, as I was more drawn to interpretation than calculation and more comfortable in subjective spaces than technical ones. Over time I realized that what I loved about the humanities was also present in math. That idea changed how I understood technical work. Numbers and equations do not stand apart from the human questions that interested me; they were just another way to interpret them. By the time I moved more seriously into applied mathematics, I saw math as the language to begin solving these human problems. That is the mindset I bring to this project. A model of heat output can explain how energy moves from a power plant into a river, but it does not automatically account for nearby communities or environmental risk. Mathematics gives me tools to study complex systems, but the non-math parts of me determine what I choose to study, and why. So when I ask, What are we optimizing for? I am no longer asking only a mathematical question. I am asking an ethical one.
